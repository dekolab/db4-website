A data story · Printing Presses and Publications Act, Malaysia

What a country bans is a diary of what it fears.

Between 1950 and 2026, Malaysia gazetted 3,213 publications as prohibited under the Printing Presses and Publications Act and the ordinances that preceded it — songbooks and pamphlets, paperbacks and cassettes. INITIATE.MY compiled, cleaned and classified every record. Read together, they trace how the grounds for banning shifted, decade by decade. This is what the record shows — and what it cannot yet say.

Part 1 · The law

What controls what Malaysians can read?

The Printing Presses and Publications Act 1984 (PPPA) [Act 301] regulates printing presses, publications and the circulation of printed materials in Malaysia. The law is intended to protect national security, public order and morality. At the same time, Article 10(1)(a) of the Federal Constitution guarantees freedom of speech and expression — although Parliament may restrict that right on grounds including security, public order and morality.

This creates an important question: how should Malaysia protect public safety without unnecessarily limiting freedom of expression?

Part 1 · The law

A law shaped by an earlier security era.

The PPPA was enacted during the first Mahathir administration and came into force on 1 September 1984. It developed from earlier systems of publication control: it replaced and absorbed the Printing Presses Act 1948, introduced during the Malayan Emergency, and the Control of Imported Publications Act 1958.

The Emergency, from 1948 to 1960, was a counterinsurgency conflict involving the Malayan Communist Party. During this period, the state expanded controls over political mobilisation, publications and materials considered subversive.

Part 1 · The law

What powers does the PPPA provide?

Under Section 7, the government may prohibit publications and control their production, importation and circulation. Enforcement can involve licensing requirements, permits, inspections, seizures and publication bans.

The official Publication Guidelines classify “undesirable publications” under seven grounds. These categories give the government significant responsibility — and considerable discretion — in deciding what materials may be circulated.

Part 1 · The law

Where is the balance?

For INITIATE.MY, the first concern is how the law balances security with fundamental rights. Materials that directly incite violence or pose a demonstrable threat to public safety may require intervention. However, restrictions should be lawful, necessary and proportionate.

Overly broad control can suppress political criticism, historical inquiry, religious discussion and controversial ideas. It may also weaken academic freedom, critical thinking and knowledge production while encouraging self-censorship.

Part 1 · The law

Why should this record be open — and why did INITIATE.MY build it?

When prohibition records are difficult to access, incomplete or inconsistently recorded, it becomes harder for the public to understand how the law has been used. Democratising the data means making it accessible, comprehensive and accurate — so that researchers, civil society organisations, students, think tanks, journalists, policymakers and Members of Parliament can take part in informed debate.

INITIATE.MY developed this database to bring together, clean and categorise prohibition data published by the Ministry of Home Affairs. It is designed as an open-source resource to:

  • support research and public understanding;
  • identify trends in publication bans;
  • encourage evidence-based policy discussions; and
  • provide recommendations for the government, legal community and civil society.

The aim is not only to document what has been banned, but to support a more open discussion about national security, public order, the rule of law and freedom of expression in Malaysia.

Part 2 · Time

Bans came in waves, not at a steady rate.

The dataset records 3,213 publication bans. The largest concentration appeared in the early 1950s — 787 bans, or 24.5% of the dataset — followed by another sustained enforcement cycle in the early-to-mid-1990s, with 715, or 22.2%. Between and after these peaks the pattern stayed uneven: 247 bans in the 1960s, 408 in the 1970s, 358 in the 1980s, 399 in the 2000s, and 229 in the 2010s.

Lower numbers after 2000 do not necessarily mean that overall content regulation declined: this dataset covers formal publication bans, not the wider regulation of online content. And the current decade — 70 bans so far — is still being written.

445 publications gazetted in 1951 alone, at the height of the Malayan Emergency — the largest single year in the record

Part 3 · Language, type & origin

Three languages carry the banned list.

Chinese is the largest recorded language category, with 1,158 entries, followed by English with 947 and Malay with 765 — together, 2,870 records. Another 263 records have no known language.

These figures show the linguistic distribution of prohibited publications, but they do not explain why the materials were banned. Language should not be treated as evidence of a particular ethnicity, ideology or type of content — and further verification, by decade, content category and official justification, is needed before conclusions are drawn about how these patterns changed over time.

Part 3 · Language, type & origin

Almost everything banned was ink on paper.

The dataset is overwhelmingly focused on print. Printed documents account for 3,148 of 3,213 records, or 98%. Audio and recordings account for 57, while physical goods, visual media and digital or electronic materials together account for only eight.

This reflects the print-based scope of the PPPA dataset — it does not mean that audiovisual or digital content was otherwise unregulated.

Part 3 · Language, type & origin

Foreign titles lead — but a third belong on no map.

Foreign-origin publications form the largest category, with 1,477 records, or 46%. Local publications account for 669, or 20.8%. And for 1,067 records — 33.2% — the origin remains unclear.

1 in 3 records has no confirmed country of origin

Because a third of the records lack a confirmed origin, comparisons between foreign and local enforcement should remain cautious.

Part 4 · The people

Who appears most often behind banned publications?

No single named author or translator dominates the dataset. Wei Wei appears most frequently with 21 bans, followed by Ustaz Ashaari Muhammad with 18 and Marcus Van Heller with 16 — the ten displayed names account for just 115 bans, a relatively dispersed pattern. Publisher data concentrates harder: Sam Luen Bookshop records 95 bans, nearly triple any other name.

95 bans record Sam Luen Bookshop as publisher — which shows repetition, not, by itself, that the publisher was deliberately targeted

Part 5 · What was banned

What were publications banned for?

The composition of publication bans changed substantially over time. In the 1950s, subversive ideological and political content accounted for 660 of 787 bans — 83.9% of the decade’s total.

By the 1990s, obscene or immoral publications had become the largest category, with 411 bans, while religious doctrinal deviance recorded 275. Religious doctrinal classifications remained substantial in the 2000s, with 194 bans, and the 2010s, with 153.

Part 5 · What was banned

Two themes tower over everything else.

Across the full dataset, erotic or immoral content is the largest subcluster, with 1,097 bans, or 34.1%. Communism or socialism follows with 850, or 26.5%. Together, these two categories account for 60.6% of all records — making moral regulation and ideological security the two largest classified themes.

Other recorded subclusters include pornography with 155 bans, Al-Arqam with 95, revolutionary politics with 78, LGBT-related content with 41 and ethnic incitement with 15. Smaller numbers should not be read as lower policy significance — their relevance depends on the period, legal basis and nature of the alleged harm.

Part 5 · What was banned

What reasons did the Ministry itself give?

Official KDN justifications are available for 1,590 of the 3,213 publications, of which only nine pre-date 1984. Two reasons dominate: morality accounts for 895 bans and public order for 654 — together, 97.4% of all recorded justifications.

Every other ground appears only rarely: causing public alarm 23, public interest nine, security five, national interest three, contrary to law one. Formal security grounds were recorded in only five cases — even though the research classifies many publications as ideological or politically subversive.

Part 5 · What was banned

The focus of enforcement migrated across the grid.

Normalise each decade to 100% and the shift is unmistakable. The 1950s were dominated by subversive ideological and political content — 660 of 787 bans. By the 1980s the focus had moved to moral and religious content: 232 obscene or immoral bans against 33 subversive and 47 religious doctrinal cases. In the 1990s, obscene or immoral publications (411) and religious doctrinal deviance (275) together accounted for 95.9% of the decade’s 715 bans.

In the 2000s the two classifications ran almost equal, at 192 and 194; religious doctrinal deviance then led the 2010s with 153 of 229 bans. The 2020s so far record 39 subversive, 27 obscene or immoral, three religious doctrinal and one 3R-related ban — an incomplete decade. These patterns reflect INITIATE.MY’s research taxonomies and do not, by themselves, prove government policy intent.

Part 5 · What was banned

The most common types of banned publication.

Two themes dominate INITIATE.MY’s taxonomies: erotic or immoral content, with 1,097 bans (34.1%), and communism or socialism, with 850 (26.5%) — together 1,947 bans, or 60.6% of the dataset. Pornography is classified separately, with 155 bans; combined with erotic or immoral content, the broader obscene or immoral cluster holds 1,252 records, although the two categories remain distinct in the research.

A further 854 publications — 26.6% — fall under “Other” or administrative and unclear grounds, limiting more specific interpretation. Below the big two sit the named campaigns: Al-Arqam (95), revolutionary politics (78), LGBT-related content (41), ethnic incitement (15), Syiah (10), Ahmadiyyah (6), terrorism or militancy (5), insults to religion (4) and royalty (3).

Part 5 · What was banned

The legal ground is not the content.

KDN’s justifications show the formal legal ground used to prohibit a publication; INITIATE.MY’s clusters describe what the publication was about. They are related but should not be treated the same.

Most publications prohibited on morality grounds were classified as obscene or immoral — 820 of 895 cases, including 701 erotic or immoral publications, 119 pornography cases and 37 LGBT-related publications. Public order was used mainly for religious doctrinal content: 565 of 654 public-order bans, including 90 Al-Arqam, seven Syiah and six Ahmadiyyah cases.

Ideological publications look different. In the post-1984 records, communism or socialism appears in only one case formally justified on security grounds, against 12 under public order. That likely understates the historical use of security justifications: most communist, socialist and revolutionary publications were banned before 1984, outside the available justification data.

Part 6 · Now, and next

How should Malaysia reform publication regulation?

Malaysia has a legitimate duty to protect national security, public order and social harmony. But publication restrictions should address clearly demonstrated harm and remain lawful, necessary, proportionate and open to review. INITIATE.MY’s four recommendations:

1

Commit to repeal — with immediate safeguards

Commit to repealing the PPPA. Until repeal, every prohibition should state its legal basis, supporting evidence, duration and appeal process. Controversy, offence or disagreement with dominant political, moral or religious views should not be enough to justify a ban.

2

Review legacy bans; add sunset clauses

The 1950s recorded 787 bans and the 1990s 715 — prohibitions that arose under different political and security conditions and should not remain in force indefinitely. Each ban should expire unless KDN can show it remains necessary and proportionate.

3

Use targeted measures before blanket bans

Depending on the harm, age classifications, content advisories, restricted access, limited redactions or action against direct incitement may be more appropriate. Political criticism, historical inquiry, religious disagreement and minority beliefs should not automatically be treated as security threats.

4

Improve data quality and public access

KDN should maintain a standardised, searchable, regularly updated database recording each publication’s title, author, publisher, language, origin, legal ground, gazette reference, review status and revocation status — with missing information, duplicates and misplaced entries clearly identified and corrected.

Coda

The record shows the grounds shifting — not the impulse fading.

Across 3,213 publications and 76 years, the through-line is a moving target: ideological security in the Emergency era, then — from the 1980s onward — moral and religious content, until the two largest classified themes, moral regulation and ideological security, account for six in ten of everything ever gazetted. The Ministry’s own stated grounds are narrower still: morality and public order cover 97.4% of recorded justifications, and formal security grounds appear in just five cases. The record has blind spots, and the analysis names them: a third of origins are unconfirmed, official justifications exist for only about half the dataset, and the current decade is unfinished. These classifications reflect research taxonomies, not proof of policy intent. And the record still moves: in July 2026, five bans were lifted. What the data shows is striking; what it cannot yet say — and what could yet change — is part of the story too.

About the data

The dataset covers 3,213 publications gazetted between 1950 and 2026, compiled from federal gazette notices published by the Ministry of Home Affairs. Fields include title, gazette date and number, type, origin, language, publisher, printer, author/translator, the Ministry’s stated justification, and a researcher-assigned cluster and subcluster. Five bans revoked in July 2026 are retained and flagged, since they were in force during the research period.

Known limitations

The origin of one-third of records (1,067 of 3,213) is unconfirmed, so foreign-versus- local comparisons should stay cautious. Official KDN justifications exist for only about half the dataset — 1,590 of 3,213 records, just nine of them pre-dating 1984 — so justification findings apply only to records that carry one. The printer field is contaminated with locations and other non-printer values and needs cleaning. And the 2020s are an incomplete decade that should not be compared directly with full ones.
