## **For more than 20 years, Shamsiah Fakeh’s memoir was openly available in Malaysia.**

Shamsiah Fakeh was a guerrilla commander, feminist and one of the most prominent Malay women in the anti-colonial movement. Her memoir offered a rare first-person account of that history. Penerbit Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM) first [published](https://www.therakyatpost.com/news/malaysia/2026/04/17/malaysia-bans-memoir-of-malay-feminist-who-fought-british-rule-two-decades-after-ukm-published-it/#gsc.tab=0) it in 2004\. It was later reprinted three times and remained in circulation for more than two decades.

## **In February 2026, it was banned.**

KDN prohibited ***Memoir Shamsiah Fakeh: Dari AWAS ke Rejimen Ke-10*** and ***Komrad Asi (Rejimen 10): Dalam Denyut Nihilisme Sejarah*** under the Printing Presses and Publications Act 1984\. The ministry said the action was intended to protect public order and cited communist elements and ideology. Gerakbudaya said it [received](https://www.therakyatpost.com/news/malaysia/2026/04/17/malaysia-bans-memoir-of-malay-feminist-who-fought-british-rule-two-decades-after-ukm-published-it/) the prohibition notice on **15 April 2026**.

KDN later said continuous surveillance and intelligence gathering had found publications containing communist elements circulating more openly, with a growing tendency to normalise and glorify the ideology. But its public explanation did not identify what had changed specifically about Shamsiah Fakeh’s memoir after more than 20 years in circulation.

## **The ban triggered public and legal pressure.**

PEN Malaysia [condemned](https://www.malaysiakini.com/letters/772763?utm_source=chatgpt.com) the decision. **Lee Chean Chung**, PKR MP for Petaling Jaya, and **Jamaliah Jamaluddin**, the Bandar Utama assemblywoman and Shamsiah Fakeh’s granddaughter, also criticised it. Gerakbudaya announced that it would challenge the prohibition. 

On **15 June 2026**, publisher **Chong Ton Sin**, owner of Gerakbudaya and the Strategic Information and Research Development Centre (SIRD), [applied](https://www.freemalaysiatoday.com/category/nation/2026/06/15/publisher-files-bid-to-quash-ban-on-cpm-memoirs) to the High Court to quash both bans. He argued that the decision was irrational because the books had already circulated for years. 

## **Four months later, the bans were gone.**

Home Minister Saifuddin Nasution Ismail signed the [revocation](https://www.freemalaysiatoday.com/category/nation/2026/08/11/home-minister-revokes-ban-on-shamsiah-fakeh-s-memoir) orders on **1 July 2026**. The bans on both memoirs were subsequently gazetted as revoked, and Chong withdrew the court challenges.

The revocations went beyond these two books. KDN gazetted **five revocation orders** also affecting *Islam Without Extremes* and *Mao Zedong: China Dalam Dunia Abad ke-20*. 

This  captures a bigger question: **What gets banned in Malaysia, why, and for how long?**

## **There are 3,212 prohibition records in the database.**

This is what is in them.

**Source:** Ministry of Home Affairs’ *Senarai Perintah Larangan*, compiled, cleaned and classified by INITIATE.MY — **3,212 recorded prohibition orders covering 1950–2026**. These are historical prohibition records and do not necessarily represent the number of publications that remain prohibited today.

Find out [how this database was built](http://127.0.0.1:5500/index.html#/methods).

## **The bans covered a wide range of religious, ideological and political materials.**

**Publications concerning Imam Khomeini** — banned because they were associated with **Syiah teachings**, which Malaysian authorities have treated as religiously sensitive or deviant.

**Mao Tse / Mao Zedong-related publications** — banned because they promoted or discussed **communist ideology**, historically treated as a political and security concern in Malaysia.

**Kahwin Cara Arqam** — banned because of its association with **Al-Arqam teachings and practices**, which were regarded by Malaysian religious authorities as deviant.

**Perak Darul Kartun** — banned because its **political cartoons and criticism of government and political leaders** were considered potentially disruptive to public order.

**Torture in Malaysia Prisons: Who You Didn’t Know and Need to Know to Act** — banned because of its **critical allegations about abuse and conditions within Malaysian prisons and security institutions**.

## **When I Was a Kid 3** — banned following controversy over content considered **racially or culturally insensitive**, particularly its portrayal of an Indonesian domestic worker.

## **Publication bans peaked in two major waves.**

Publication bans occurred in waves rather than at a steady rate. The largest concentration appeared in the **early 1950s**, followed by another sustained enforcement cycle in the **early-to-mid-1990s**.

The 1950s recorded **787 bans, or 24.5% of the dataset**, while the 1990s recorded **715, or 22.3%**. Between these two peaks, the pattern remained uneven: **247 bans** were recorded in the 1960s, **408** in the 1970s, **358** in the 1980s and **399** in the 2000s.

The 2010s recorded **229 bans**, while the 2020s have recorded **70 so far**. As the current decade is incomplete, it should not be compared directly with full decades. Lower numbers after 2000 also do not necessarily mean that overall content regulation declined, as this dataset captures formal publication bans rather than the wider regulation of digital and online content.

## **Chinese, English and Malay dominate the recorded language distribution.**

Chinese is the largest category, with **1,158 entries**, followed by English with **947** and Malay with **765**. Together, they account for **2,870 records — nearly nine in ten publications in the database**.

The pattern also reflects some historical trends. **Chinese-language publications are strongly associated with earlier communist and socialist material**, while English and Malay publications appear across a wider range of political, religious and moral categories. Language alone, however, does not determine why a publication was banned.

The dataset also contains **263 records with an unknown language**, limiting more precise conclusions about changes over time.

## **Publications bans overwhelmingly targeted print, with nearly half involving foreign-origin materials.**

Printed documents account for **3,148 of 3,212 records, or 98%**. Audio and recordings account for **57**, while physical goods, visual media and digital or electronic materials together account for only **eight**. This reflects the print-based scope of the PPPA dataset, but does not mean audiovisual or digital content was otherwise unregulated.

Foreign-origin publications form the largest category, with **1,477 records, or 46%**. Local publications account for **669, or 20.8%**, while the origin of **1,067 records, or 33.2%**, remains unclear. Because one-third of the records lack a confirmed origin, comparisons between foreign and local enforcement should remain cautious.

## **Authors are dispersed, but some publishers appear repeatedly.**

No single named author or translator dominates the dataset. **Wei Wei** appears most frequently, with **21 bans**, followed by **Ustaz Ashaari Muhammad with 18** and **Marcus Van Heller with 16**. The ten most frequently listed names account for only **115 bans**, showing a relatively dispersed pattern.

**Yayasan Perkhabaran Injil** is the most frequently listed printer, with **35 bans**. All other displayed printer entries record **17 or fewer**.

Publisher data shows greater concentration. **Sam Luen Bookshop appears in 95 bans**, followed by **Yayasan Perkhabaran Injil with 35**. Every other displayed publisher records **21 or fewer**.

These repeated appearances show that some publishers were associated with multiple prohibited publications, but they do not by themselves prove deliberate targeting. Higher counts may also reflect publication volume, repeated imports, multiple editions or inconsistent naming.

## **Morality and public order dominate KDN’s recorded justifications.**

Official KDN justifications are available for **1,590 of the 3,212 publications** in the dataset. Only **nine** of these records pre-date 1984\.

Two legal grounds dominate. **Morality** accounts for **895 bans**, while **public order** accounts for **654**. Together, they represent **97.4% of all available justifications**.

Other grounds were used rarely: **causing public alarm (23), public interest (9), security (5), national interest (3)** and **contrary to law (1)**.

These findings apply only to publications with a recorded KDN justification. Because justifications are available for only about half of the dataset and are overwhelmingly post-1984, they do not represent the complete historical pattern.

## **Three broad content themes account for 90.6% of banned publications.**

INITIATE.MY classified the **3,212 records** into five broad content clusters. The largest is **obscene or immoral content**, with **1,252 records (39.0%)**, including erotic or immoral material and pornography.

**Subversive ideological and political content** follows with **974 records (30.3%)**, covering communism or socialism, revolutionary politics, LGBT-related content, and terrorism or militancy. **Religious doctrinal deviance** accounts for **684 records (21.3%)**, including publications associated with Al-Arqam, Syiah, Ahmadiyyah and other religious teachings.

**Race, religion and royalty (3R) issues** form the smallest cluster, with **22 records (0.7%)**. A further **281 records (8.7%)** are classified as **general or unidentified** because there was insufficient information for reliable classification, including vague publications from the Malayan Emergency and communist insurgency periods, possible nationalist materials and one-word titles.

Together, the three largest clusters account for **90.6% of the dataset**. These categories describe the apparent content of publications under INITIATE.MY’s research taxonomy. 

## **Erotic or immoral content and communism or socialism account for 60.6% of banned publications.**

The subcluster analysis identifies the more specific types of content within the broader taxonomy. **Erotic or immoral content** is the largest subcluster, with **1,097 bans (34.1%)**, followed by **communism or socialism** with **850 (26.5%)**. Together, they account for **1,947 records, or 60.6% of the dataset**.

**Other religious doctrinal teachings** is the third-largest subcluster, with **573 bans (17.8%)**, covering religious publications not classified under Al-Arqam, Syiah or Ahmadiyyah.

A further **281 records (8.7%)** are **general or unidentified** because available information was insufficient for more precise classification. **Pornography** accounts for **155 bans (4.8%)** and is classified separately from erotic or immoral content.

The remaining subclusters are **Al-Arqam (95), revolutionary politics (78), LGBT-related content (41), ethnic incitement (15), Syiah (10), Ahmadiyyah (6), terrorism or militancy (5), insults to religion (4)** and **insults to royalty (3)**.

## **KDN’s legal grounds and INITIATE.MY’s content classifications overlap, but not consistently.**

Three findings stand out. **Morality** was strongly associated with obscene or immoral content: **820 of 895 morality-based bans** fell within this cluster, including **701 erotic or immoral publications** and **119 pornography cases**.

**Public order** was closely associated with religious doctrinal content. Of **654 public-order bans**, **565** involved religious doctrinal deviance, including **90 Al-Arqam, seven Syiah and six Ahmadiyyah publications**.

By contrast, **ideological content did not consistently map onto security grounds**. Communism or socialism appeared in only **one security case**, compared with **12 public-order cases** in the available justification data. This should be interpreted cautiously because official reasons are largely unavailable for pre-1984 bans.

Overall, the comparison shows that **similar types of content could be prohibited under different legal grounds**, reinforcing the distinction between what a publication contains and the legal reason used to ban it.

## **Publication regulation should protect against harm, not simply controversial ideas.**

**Section 7(1) of the PPPA** gives the Home Minister particularly broad authority. Where the Minister is satisfied that a publication may prejudice **public order, morality, security, public or national interest**, among other grounds, the Act states that the Minister may act **“in his absolute discretion”** to prohibit its printing, importation, publication, sale, circulation, distribution or possession.

Such broad discretion should be balanced against **freedom of expression under Article 10(1)(a) of the Federal Constitution**. Restrictions should therefore be based on **clear evidence of harm, necessity and proportionality**.

## **The PPPA should be repealed, with stronger safeguards introduced immediately.**

The PPPA reflects an earlier system of publication control shaped partly by the security concerns of the Malayan Emergency.

Until repeal, **Section 7(1)’s broad ministerial discretion should be narrowed and subject to stronger safeguards**. Every prohibition should clearly state its **specific reason and legal ground, supporting evidence, duration, review process and right of appeal**.

Political criticism, historical inquiry, religious disagreement or controversial views should not, by themselves, justify a ban.

## **Blanket bans should be a last resort.**

Not every harmful publication requires complete prohibition.

Authorities should first consider **age classifications, content advisories, restricted access, limited redactions or targeted action against direct incitement**.

This would address genuine harm while reducing unnecessary restrictions on political debate, academic freedom and access to knowledge.

## **Old bans should be reviewed, and their current status made clear.**

The database records **787 bans from the 1950s and 715 from the 1990s**, many imposed under very different political and security conditions. KDN should introduce **periodic reviews and sunset clauses**.

KDN should also create a **consolidated prohibition and revocation register** linking each ban to any subsequent *Perintah Mesin Cetak dan Penerbitan (Kawalan Hasil Penerbitan Tidak Diingini) (Pembatalan)* or court decision.

Each record should clearly state whether it is **Active, Revoked, Quashed by Court, Under Review or Status Unclear**.

## **Better regulation requires better public data.**

KDN should maintain a **searchable and regularly updated database** showing each publication’s title, author, publisher, legal ground, gazette reference and current status.

Open data would allow **researchers, journalists, civil society, policymakers, Members of Parliament and the public** to scrutinise publication regulation and participate in evidence-based reform.
